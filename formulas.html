Classical Mechanics

Newton's Second Law

F = m · a

The net force on an object equals its mass times its acceleration. Push harder, it accelerates more; the same push on something heavier accelerates it less.

  • F - net force (newtons, N)
  • m - mass (kilograms, kg)
  • a - acceleration (m/s²)

Derivation

Momentum is p = mv. Newton's original statement is that force is the rate of change of momentum: F = dp/dt. For an object whose mass doesn't change, this simplifies to F = m(dv/dt) = ma.

Applications

  • Sizing rocket engines against payload mass
  • Calculating a vehicle's braking distance
  • Structural engineering load calculations
Fluid Dynamics

Bernoulli's Equation

P + ½ρv² + ρgh = constant

Along a streamline, as a fluid speeds up, its pressure drops. This is one of the two classic explanations for lift: air moving faster over a wing's curved upper surface has lower pressure than the slower air beneath it.

  • P - static pressure
  • ρ - fluid (air) density
  • v - flow speed
  • h - height (usually negligible for air)

Derivation

Apply conservation of energy to a small parcel of fluid moving along a streamline, treating pressure as a form of energy per unit volume. For steady, incompressible, frictionless flow, the sum of pressure energy, kinetic energy, and gravitational potential energy per unit volume stays constant.

Applications

  • Explaining lift generation over a wing's curved surface
  • Airspeed indicators (pitot-static systems)
  • Venturi-effect carburetors and flow meters
Aeronautics

The Lift Equation

L = ½ρv²SC_L

The single most important equation in aeronautics. Every aircraft, from a paper plane to an airliner, stays up because this equation produces a force equal to its weight.

  • L - lift force
  • ρ - air density
  • v - airspeed
  • S - wing area
  • C_L - lift coefficient (depends on wing shape and angle of attack)

Derivation (conceptual)

Lift comes from the pressure difference between a wing's upper and lower surfaces, integrated over the whole wing area. Since that pressure difference scales with dynamic pressure (½ρv²), the messy details of exact pressure distribution get folded into a single empirically or computationally determined number: the lift coefficient, C_L.

Applications

  • Sizing a wing for a given aircraft weight and cruise speed
  • Calculating stall speed at any given weight and configuration
  • Predicting how lift changes with altitude, speed, or flap setting
Fluid Dynamics

Navier–Stokes Equations

ρ(∂u/∂t + u·∇u) = −∇p + μ∇²u + f

Newton's second law applied to a moving fluid: inertia on the left, pressure and viscous forces (plus any external force) on the right.

  • u - fluid velocity field
  • ρ - fluid density
  • p - pressure
  • μ - dynamic viscosity
  • f - external body forces (e.g. gravity)

Derivation

Apply conservation of momentum to an infinitesimal parcel of fluid, then add a constitutive relation describing how viscous stress depends on the velocity gradient. The result is a nonlinear partial differential equation - famously still without a general proof of smooth solutions in three dimensions (a Millennium Prize problem).

Applications

  • Full computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulation of airflow over an aircraft
  • Modeling turbulence and boundary layer separation on a wing
  • Engine intake and exhaust flow design
Propulsion

Thrust Equation (Momentum Theory)

T = ṁ(V_e - V_0)

Every engine, propeller or jet, works the same fundamental way: accelerate a mass of air or exhaust backward, and Newton's third law pushes the aircraft forward.

  • T - thrust force
  • ṁ - mass flow rate of air or exhaust
  • V_e - exhaust (or propeller slipstream) velocity
  • V_0 - inlet velocity (the aircraft's own airspeed)

Derivation

This comes directly from Newton's second law written in terms of momentum: force equals the rate of change of momentum. An engine takes in air at the aircraft's speed and expels it faster; the rate at which that momentum change happens, mass flow times the change in velocity, is the reaction force felt as thrust.

Applications

  • Comparing turbojet, turbofan, and propeller engine performance
  • Sizing an engine for a required thrust at cruise or takeoff
  • Understanding why high-bypass turbofans are more fuel-efficient than turbojets
