Formula Library

Five equations that shaped physics. Each one includes what it means, what every symbol stands for, where it comes from, and where it's actually used.

Classical Mechanics

Newton's Second Law

F = m · a

The net force on an object equals its mass times its acceleration. Push harder, it accelerates more; the same push on something heavier accelerates it less.

  • F - net force (newtons, N)
  • m - mass (kilograms, kg)
  • a - acceleration (m/s²)

Derivation

Momentum is p = mv. Newton's original statement is that force is the rate of change of momentum: F = dp/dt. For an object whose mass doesn't change, this simplifies to F = m(dv/dt) = ma.

Applications

  • Sizing rocket engines against payload mass
  • Calculating a vehicle's braking distance
  • Structural engineering load calculations
Electromagnetism

Maxwell's Equations

∇·E = ρ/ε₀
∇·B = 0
∇×E = −∂B/∂t
∇×B = μ₀J + μ₀ε₀∂E/∂t

Four equations that fully describe classical electricity and magnetism - how charges and currents create fields, and how changing fields create each other. Light itself falls out of these equations as a self-sustaining wave.

  • E, B - electric and magnetic field vectors
  • ρ - electric charge density
  • J - electric current density
  • ε₀, μ₀ - permittivity and permeability of free space

Derivation

Each equation started as a separate experimental law - Gauss's law for electric fields, Gauss's law for magnetism, Faraday's law of induction, and Ampère's law. Maxwell unified them and added the "displacement current" term, which made the set of equations consistent with charge conservation and predicted electromagnetic waves.

Applications

  • Antenna and wireless communication design
  • Fiber optics and photonics
  • MRI machine field design
Quantum Mechanics

Schrödinger Equation

iħ ∂Ψ/∂t = ĤΨ

Describes how a quantum system's wavefunction - which encodes the probabilities of every measurable outcome - changes over time.

  • Ψ - the wavefunction, encoding the quantum state
  • ħ - reduced Planck constant (h / 2π)
  • Ĥ - the Hamiltonian operator (total energy of the system)
  • t - time

Derivation (conceptual)

Schrödinger sought an equation whose plane-wave solutions matched the de Broglie relations (E = ħω, p = ħk) while conserving the classical energy relation E = p²/2m + V. Substituting the wave-operator forms of E and p into that energy relation produces the equation.

Applications

  • Predicting atomic orbital shapes
  • Semiconductor band-structure design
  • Quantum computing gate design
Fluid Dynamics

Navier–Stokes Equations

ρ(∂u/∂t + u·∇u) = −∇p + μ∇²u + f

Newton's second law applied to a moving fluid: inertia on the left, pressure and viscous forces (plus any external force) on the right.

  • u - fluid velocity field
  • ρ - fluid density
  • p - pressure
  • μ - dynamic viscosity
  • f - external body forces (e.g. gravity)

Derivation

Apply conservation of momentum to an infinitesimal parcel of fluid, then add a constitutive relation describing how viscous stress depends on the velocity gradient. The result is a nonlinear partial differential equation - famously still without a general proof of smooth solutions in three dimensions (a Millennium Prize problem).

Applications

  • Aircraft and vehicle aerodynamics
  • Weather and ocean current modeling
  • Blood flow simulation
General Relativity

Einstein Field Equations

Gμν + Λgμν = (8πG/c⁴) Tμν

Matter and energy (right side) determine the curvature of spacetime (left side) - and that curvature, in turn, is what we experience as gravity.

  • Gμν - Einstein tensor, describing spacetime curvature
  • Λ - the cosmological constant
  • gμν - the metric tensor, defining distances in spacetime
  • Tμν - the stress-energy tensor, describing matter and energy
  • G, c - the gravitational constant and speed of light

Derivation (conceptual)

Einstein required a tensor equation linking curvature to energy-momentum that automatically respected local conservation of energy and momentum, and that reduced to Newton's law of gravity in the weak-field, low-speed limit. The Einstein tensor is the unique (up to the cosmological constant) geometric object satisfying both conditions.

Applications

  • GPS satellite clock corrections
  • Predicting gravitational lensing around massive objects
  • Modeling black holes and the expansion of the universe
